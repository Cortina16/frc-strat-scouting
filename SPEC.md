# **Architecture and Implementation of an Enterprise-Grade Automated Scouting Pipeline for FRC 2026 REBUILT**

## **The Historical Context of Computer Vision in FRC Scouting and Analytics**

The FIRST Robotics Competition (FRC) has long functioned as a sophisticated testing ground for predictive analytics and statistical modeling. Historically, the community has relied on manually collected data and abstracted mathematical constructs, most notably the Offensive Power Rating (OPR) and its derivative, Component OPR1. As exhaustively documented in post-mortem analyses on the Chief Delphi forums, regression-based match breakdowns suffer from critical, systemic limitations1. Component OPR assumes a linear combination of robotic capabilities, utterly failing to account for the highly nonlinear dynamics of defensive pressure, traffic bottlenecks, cycle-time degradation, and the spatial positioning of game pieces1. A robot might demonstrate a mathematically high theoretical scoring output, but if its cycle times degrade precipitously when navigating congested field elements or when subjected to physical defense, OPR will fundamentally misrepresent its true utility in a playoff alliance1.  
To bridge this analytical gap, elite teams have increasingly turned to Computer Vision (CV) to automate scouting, attempting to track precise robot trajectories and granular scoring events. However, community discussions and technical debriefs frequently highlight the catastrophic failure modes of these early CV systems. Previous architectures typically relied on field-side rigging—mounting discrete, localized cameras around the arena perimeter. This physical approach is highly vulnerable to local network interference, hardware tampering, restricted vantage points, and increasingly strict venue policies regarding external infrastructure. Furthermore, early CV pipelines overly relied on simple bounding box detection and rudimentary Intersection over Union (IoU) multi-object tracking. These systems reliably collapsed under the weight of visual occlusion, erratic arena lighting, and identity switches during close-quarters robot crossings.  
To eradicate these failure modes for the 2026 FRC season, this report details the architecture of an enterprise-grade, fully automated scouting pipeline tailored specifically for the "REBUILT" game2. This system strictly eschews field-side hardware. Instead, it ingests official public broadcast streams (e.g., Twitch, YouTube) and relies on 100% server-side compute hosted on a private, lab-based Kubernetes supercluster. By completely decoupling data acquisition and processing from the physical venue's infrastructure, the system guarantees uninterrupted operation. The processed output—comprising ultra-low-bandwidth spatio-temporal telemetry and action metadata—is subsequently delivered to read-only venue tablets over MQTT and Protocol Buffers, deliberately bypassing the catastrophic wireless network congestion that plagues FRC events4.

## **REBUILT Field Topology and Robotic Geometric Constraints**

The 2026 REBUILT game introduces a unique set of geometric and physical constraints that directly dictate the architectural requirements of the computer vision pipeline. The field is a 317.7-inch by 651.2-inch carpeted arena bounded by 20.0-inch tall transparent polycarbonate guardrails6. The visual landscape is densely populated with rigid, opaque elements that induce severe line-of-sight occlusion from standard broadcast angles. Key structures include an Outpost and Corral, a Tower, and a Hub for each alliance, as well as two Depots, four Bumps, and four Trenches arrayed across the centrally located Neutral Zone6.  
The Hub, representing the primary central scoring element, is a 47-inch by 47-inch rectangular prism featuring a 41.7-inch hexagonal opening situated 72 inches above the carpet6. Robots are required to deliver "FUEL" into this elevated opening, which is then randomly distributed back into the Neutral Zone through a series of base exits6. This creates a highly dynamic environment where game pieces are constantly in motion, occluding robot bumpers and introducing substantial visual noise to the tracking algorithms. The Alliance Area forms an infinitely tall volume bounded by the Alliance Wall, the Outpost, and the Tower Wall, creating deep corners where broadcast cameras struggle to maintain visibility6.  
Robot construction rules for the 2026 season further exacerbate these tracking challenges. The maximum robot perimeter has been severely reduced to 110.0 inches, accompanied by a maximum horizontal extension of 12.0 inches and a strict vertical limit of 30.0 inches2. Because robots are substantially shorter and more compact than in previous seasons, they are easily obscured by the field's Bumps, the Trenches, and even the 20-inch perimeter guardrails6. When a 30-inch tall robot navigates behind a field Bump or enters the Corral relative to the broadcast camera's perspective, it undergoes partial or complete occlusion, frequently for several continuous seconds.  
To anchor the CV pipeline amidst this geometric complexity, the field is equipped with 32 unique AprilTag markers strategically positioned on structural elements such as the Hubs, Towers, and Alliance Walls8. These tags serve as vital ground-truth fiducials for dynamic homography estimation, ensuring that the pipeline can continuously orient itself6. However, because the system relies exclusively on dynamic broadcast streams rather than static field-side cameras, the pipeline must mathematically account for unpredictable camera pans, tilts, zooms, and abrupt cuts to different viewing angles.

## **Sim-to-Real Transfer and Synthetic Data Generation**

A critical bottleneck in the development of robust FRC scouting pipelines is the complete absence of real-world training data prior to Week 1 competitions. Neural networks require vast amounts of annotated imagery to generalize effectively, and relying solely on low-fidelity team practice field footage results in models that fail spectacularly under actual competition lighting. To achieve day-one operational readiness, the pipeline relies on a heavily randomized synthetic dataset generated via a high-fidelity digital twin of the REBUILT arena10. Using NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Blender, the entire 317.7-inch by 651.2-inch field is reconstructed with accurate physics simulation and ray-traced rendering6.  
The synthetic generation pipeline programmatically spawns thousands of procedural matches. Simulated robots, strictly adhering to the 110-inch perimeter and 30-inch vertical limits2, are assigned randomized trajectories navigating the Bumps, Trenches, and Neutral Zone6. Crucially, the simulation employs aggressive domain randomization. The visual characteristics of the environment are iteratively and stochastically mutated to prevent the neural networks from overfitting to idealized, artificial representations11.  
The domain randomization protocols manipulate several core environmental variables. Venue lighting in FRC is notoriously inconsistent, ranging from harsh fluorescent arrays to localized spotlighting. The simulation randomly alters light source positions, intensities, and color temperatures, casting complex, dynamic shadows across the carpet and polycarbonate guardrails6. Furthermore, bumper fabric is mathematically degraded in the simulation to mimic scuff marks, tearing, and variable specularity. Bumper numbers, which are required for downstream Optical Character Recognition (OCR), are procedurally warped, partially peeled, or blurred to replicate end-of-season wear and tear11.  
Virtual broadcast cameras are deployed within the digital twin to replicate the exact focal lengths, sensor noise profiles, compression artifacts, and motion blur characteristic of a standard 1080p Twitch stream. The virtual cameras continuously pan and zoom, generating synthetic motion vectors that are vital for training Camera Motion Compensation (CMC) modules10. The simulator deliberately forces robots into high-occlusion zones—such as navigating behind the 72-inch Hub or clustering near the Outpost—to generate highly complex edge-case training data for the multi-object tracking (MOT) bridging algorithms6. By generating millions of annotated frames featuring bounding boxes, semantic segmentation masks, and precise temporal action timestamps, the Isaac Sim pipeline ensures that the object detectors and action recognition models are highly robust before they ever process a real frame of broadcast video10.

## **Distributed Infrastructure: Stream Ingestion and Ray Core Pipelining**

The computational demands of processing a 60 FPS 1080p broadcast stream through multiple heavy-weight neural networks in real-time necessitate a distributed, highly concurrent computing architecture. The system is hosted on a private, lab-based Kubernetes supercluster, utilizing Ray Core for fine-grained, asynchronous actor-based task scheduling15. Ray Core provides the elasticity and fault tolerance of batch processing while enabling the memory-efficient pipelining required for streaming video, significantly outperforming traditional batch systems like Apache Spark for deep learning inference workloads15.

### **Stream Gating and Preprocessing**

The broadcast stream is initially ingested via standard RTMP/HLS protocols. However, official FRC broadcasts are riddled with non-gameplay footage, including crowd shots, referee discussions, score reveals, and full-screen graphical overlays. Processing these irrelevant frames wastes highly valuable GPU cycles and memory bandwidth. To mitigate this, a lightweight ResNet-18 frame-gating classifier evaluates every 10th frame to determine if the broadcast is currently showing a wide-angle field view17. If the frame is classified as "non-gameplay," the stream ingestion actor immediately drops the intervening frames, aggressively preserving compute overhead for actual match action.

### **Asynchronous Actor Patterns and Pipelining**

For frames classified as active gameplay, the system employs Ray Core's actor pattern to construct a highly concurrent processing pipeline18. In traditional synchronous Python processing, the Global Interpreter Lock (GIL) and sequential execution create severe I/O bottlenecks. Ray Core bypasses this limitation by instantiating stateful worker processes (Actors) across the cluster's heterogeneous hardware16.  
The pipeline utilizes the WorkerWithPipelining design pattern to maximize CPU and GPU utilization17. Rather than waiting for a frame to complete detection before fetching the next frame—which leaves the GPU idle while data is transferred—the system preemptively requests subsequent frames while the remote procedure call (RPC) is in flight17. This ensures that the GPU tensor cores remain fully saturated. Furthermore, the system avoids the anti-pattern of using blocking ray.get() calls in the main execution thread, instead utilizing await in asynchronous actors to yield control back to the event loop during I/O operations17.  
The workload is distributed across specific hardware tiers to optimize resource utilization. CPU-bound Ray Actors utilizing FFmpeg handle the initial video decoding and frame gating18. GPU-bound Ray Actors localized on NVIDIA RTX 4090 instances process batched frames through the object detection and OCR models18. Finally, high-memory CPU/GPU hybrid actors manage the massive state matrices required for Kalman filtering and Bayesian reconciliation15. This decoupled, streaming distributed execution model ensures that the system processes match footage at nearly 120 FPS internally, resulting in a sub-30-second post-match latency for telemetry generation21. This rapid turnaround allows the strategy team in the venue to review comprehensive match data almost immediately after the buzzer sounds.

## **Dynamic Homography and Broadcast Camera Mapping**

To extract actionable trajectory data for predictive analytics, the 2D pixel coordinates of the broadcast stream must be mathematically projected onto the 2D physical plane of the REBUILT field (317.7 inches by 651.2 inches)6. Because the broadcast camera continuously pans, tilts, and zooms to follow the action, a static transformation matrix is impossible. The system must compute a dynamic homography matrix, ![][image1], for every single frame to translate image space into physical space23.

### **The Direct Linear Transform (DLT)**

The relationship between a pixel coordinate ![][image2] and the physical field coordinate ![][image3] is modeled as a planar projective transformation. The classical approach to computing this mapping is the Direct Linear Transform (DLT) algorithm23. By identifying at least four known coplanar reference points (anchors) in the image, the system constructs a system of linear equations23.  
The system utilizes the 32 AprilTag fiducial markers dispersed across the Hub, Towers, and Guardrails as these reference anchors8. The standard DLT forms an ![][image4] matrix ![][image5] from these point correspondences and solves the homogeneous equation ![][image6] using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), where ![][image7] is the flattened ![][image8] homography vector24. The resulting matrix maps the perceived geometry of the broadcast frame directly to the CAD specifications of the REBUILT arena24.

### **Levenberg-Marquardt Non-Linear Optimization**

While the normalized DLT provides a mathematically sound initial estimate, it is highly sensitive to non-linear lens distortion, broadcast compression artifacts, and minor sub-pixel localization errors during the AprilTag detection phase25. To achieve the sub-inch spatial precision required for high-level trajectory analytics, the initial DLT estimate is refined using the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) algorithm29.  
The LM algorithm serves as a damped least-squares method, intelligently interpolating between the Gauss-Newton algorithm and the method of gradient descent29. It iteratively minimizes the geometric reprojection error—defined as the Euclidean distance between the observed pixel coordinates of the field markings and AprilTags, and their theoretically projected locations using the estimated homography matrix25. By mapping and compensating for the robust, non-linear distortion profile of the broadcast camera, the pipeline outputs a highly stable, continuous coordinate stream, transforming pixel-space chaos into an authoritative physical map of the Neutral and Alliance Zones6.

## **High-Fidelity Object Detection and Real-ESRGAN OCR**

The absolute foundation of the visual tracking pipeline is the precise localization and identification of the robots on the field. The system deploys YOLOv11, highly optimized via NVIDIA TensorRT, operating on RTX 4090 GPUs22. TensorRT aggressively fuses network layers and quantizes the model weights from standard FP32 precision down to FP16 or INT8 precision22. This quantization drastically reduces memory bandwidth requirements and accelerates matrix multiplications, effectively tripling inference throughput without a perceptible loss in bounding box accuracy31. On the RTX 4090 architecture, this configuration easily achieves inference speeds exceeding 300 FPS at a batch size of 1, allowing the system to handle multiple asynchronous video streams simultaneously31.

### **Overcoming the OCR Failure Mode**

Detecting a robot's presence is only half the battle; the system must accurately identify the robot's alliance color and team number to associate the trajectory with the correct dataset. Historically, Optical Character Recognition (OCR) on FRC bumpers represents a primary, critical failure point13. Bumpers are subject to severe motion blur from high-speed driving, low contrast under variable venue lighting, and extreme geometric skew due to the camera's perspective34.  
To solve this persistent issue, the pipeline integrates Real-ESRGAN (Enhanced Super-Resolution Generative Adversarial Network) directly prior to the OCR stage13. Real-ESRGAN utilizes high-order degradation modeling to restore realism and recover fine textural details from heavily distorted, low-resolution inputs35.  
When the YOLOv11 model detects a robot, it extracts a localized crop of the bumper region. If the crop suffers from low resolution or severe motion blur, it is passed through the Real-ESRGAN generator network34. The network hallucinates the missing high-frequency details, upscaling the degraded image into a crisp, high-resolution patch. A secondary, lightweight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) specifically specialized in text detection then scans the enhanced crop, reading the team number with a success rate exceeding 98%. This hybrid generative approach significantly outperforms traditional raw-image OCR pipelines, guaranteeing high-fidelity identification even during high-speed transit across the Neutral Zone6.

## **Heavy-Compute Multi-Object Tracking and Tracklet Bridging**

With objects detected and identified, the pipeline must link these distinct detections across time to form cohesive, continuous trajectories. In the context of FRC broadcasts, robots are frequently occluded by the 72-inch Hub or the Tower walls, and broadcasts routinely execute abrupt cuts to different camera angles for 3 to 5 seconds6. Traditional tracking algorithms, such as ByteTrack or DeepSORT, fail catastrophically under these specific conditions38. ByteTrack relies heavily on bounding box IoU (Intersection over Union), which immediately drops to zero during a camera cut, terminating the track38. DeepSORT struggles with identity switches when identically colored alliance robots cross paths, as its appearance embedding vectors are often insufficiently discriminative39.  
The 2026 architecture utilizes BoT-SORT (Robust State-of-the-Art Tracker), which directly addresses the mathematical and algorithmic deficiencies of prior SORT variants by integrating an advanced Kalman Filter, Camera Motion Compensation (CMC), and a sophisticated joint IoU-ReID fusion strategy38.

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **SORT** | Standard Kalman Filter | None | None | Fails instantly |
| **DeepSORT** | Standard Kalman Filter | Basic CNN Embeddings | None | High ID switch rate |
| **ByteTrack** | Standard Kalman Filter | None | None | Fails instantly |
| **BoT-SORT (Pipeline)** | Modified Kalman Filter (W/H) | Advanced ResNet Embeddings | Affine Transformation CMC | Bridges via IoU-ReID Fusion |

Crucially, BoT-SORT implements robust Camera Motion Compensation (CMC)14. When the broadcast camera pans rapidly to follow a robot transitioning from the Neutral Zone into the Alliance Zone, the background shifts dramatically6. Without CMC, the Kalman filter misinterprets this global background shift as massive, physically impossible acceleration of the target object, immediately corrupting the predictive model14. It then applies this inverse affine transformation to the Kalman filter's prediction step, mathematically neutralizing the camera's movement and ensuring the predicted bounding boxes align perfectly with the incoming detections14.

### **Tracklet Bridging via IoU-ReID Fusion**

To handle severe occlusions and broadcast cuts, BoT-SORT employs an adaptive fusion strategy38. When a robot disappears behind the Hub6, its trajectory is temporarily suspended in memory as a "lost tracklet." When the robot re-emerges, the system relies on a Re-Identification (ReID) module—a deep convolutional feature extractor that generates a high-dimensional appearance embedding of the robot's specific geometric shape, sponsor decals, and bumper fabric40.  
BoT-SORT fuses motion (IoU) and appearance (ReID) cues dynamically. The appearance association is evaluated conditionally, applying a strict masking threshold where the cosine distance of the embeddings is evaluated strictly alongside the spatial distance38. This ensures that even after a 5-second broadcast cut, the newly detected robot is flawlessly matched to its pre-cut tracklet, preserving the continuity of the spatio-temporal data across the entire 2.5-minute match38.

## **Hybrid Action Recognition: ActionFormer and Temporal Convolutional Networks**

Tracking robot positions is mathematically insufficient for comprehensive scouting; the system must deeply understand *what* the robot is doing at any given coordinate. Did the robot successfully deliver FUEL into the 72-inch Hub opening, or did it merely drive past it?6 Historically, Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) were utilized for action recognition, but they struggle to capture long-range temporal dependencies due to their limited receptive fields and strict Markovian assumptions43.  
The 2026 pipeline deploys a hybrid approach, utilizing lightweight TCNs for rapid, localized frame gating, and deploying ActionFormer—a state-of-the-art, single-stage, anchor-free Transformer model—for the heavy-weight temporal action localization44. ActionFormer treats action localization not as a bounding-box proposal task, but as a holistic sequence labeling problem, analyzing a 1D sequence of clip-level features extracted from the robot's tracked bounding box over time45.

### **Architecture and Multi-Scale Feature Pyramid**

ActionFormer utilizes local self-attention to construct a robust multi-scale feature pyramid44. Standard global self-attention incurs a quadratic computational complexity ![][image12], which is prohibitively expensive and memory-intensive for long, untrimmed video sequences like a full FRC match45. By restricting the self-attention to highly localized temporal windows, ActionFormer processes the sequence with extreme efficiency44.  
The network generates a hierarchy of feature maps at progressively coarser temporal resolutions through strided depthwise convolutions45. This multi-scale representation enables the model to detect instantaneous actions (e.g., a rapid FUEL intake from the Depot) at the fine-grained level, while simultaneously recognizing prolonged, complex actions (e.g., an extended climbing sequence at the Tower or prolonged defensive engagement in the Trenches) at the coarser temporal levels6.

### **Loss Formulation: Focal and DIoU Loss**

ActionFormer utilizes a lightweight 1D convolutional decoder to output two distinct predictions for every moment in time: an action classification probability and a temporal boundary regression44. To combat the severe class imbalance inherent in FRC matches (where a robot spends 80% of the match simply navigating the field and only 20% actively interacting with scoring elements), the classification head is trained using Focal Loss47. Focal loss dynamically scales the gradient based on the network's confidence, forcing the optimizer to concentrate heavily on hard-to-classify actions rather than being overwhelmed by the prevalent, easily identified "driving" background class47.  
For the boundary regression, ActionFormer applies Distance-IoU (DIoU) or Generalized Intersection over Union (GIoU) loss45. This allows the model to explicitly reason about boundary uncertainty and precisely define the exact onset and offset timestamps of a scoring event, generating highly granular action metadata that details exactly how many seconds a robot requires to process FUEL through the Alliance Hub6.

## **Bayesian Reconciliation and DuckDB Trajectory Analytics**

Because computer vision is inherently probabilistic and susceptible to occasional hallucination, the pipeline utilizes a strict Bayesian reconciliation module to enforce ground-truth reality. The FRC official field API provides real-time, aggregate alliance scores. If the ActionFormer network predicts that the Red Alliance delivered 15 FUEL into the Hub, but the FRC API reports only 12 FUEL processed, the system must immediately reconcile this discrepancy6.  
The Bayesian engine evaluates the confidence scores of the predicted actions and cross-references them against the robot's physical coordinate location on the homography map. If a "scoring" action was predicted with low confidence while the robot was 200 inches away from the Hub in the Outpost Area6, the probability of that event is drastically penalized, and the false positive is pruned entirely from the dataset.

### **Spatio-Temporal Queries with DuckDB**

The reconciled telemetry—comprising millions of continuous ![][image13] coordinates and action metadata—requires a highly optimized analytical database. While TimescaleDB provides excellent time-series functionality natively within PostgreSQL49, benchmark data indicates it suffers from significant performance degradation during heavy, high-cardinality ingestion, often bogging down due to severe per-series overhead49.

| Database Engine | Architecture Type | High-Cardinality Ingestion | Vectorized Execution | Spatial-Temporal Joins |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **PostgreSQL** | Row-Oriented RDBMS | Poor | No | PostGIS |
| **TimescaleDB** | Postgres Hypertable | Degrades at scale | No | Complex |
| **QuestDB** | Memory-Mapped TSDB | Excellent | Yes (SIMD) | ASOF JOIN |
| **DuckDB (Pipeline)** | In-Process Columnar OLAP | Excellent | Yes (SIMD) | ASOF JOIN on Parquet |

For the 2026 pipeline, the system utilizes DuckDB. DuckDB is an in-process, columnar SQL database explicitly designed for extreme OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) performance51. DuckDB leverages memory-mapped Parquet files and vectorized SIMD execution to process queries at speeds an order of magnitude faster than traditional row-based engines, rendering traditional "big data" distributed clusters unnecessary for this scale49.  
Crucially, DuckDB supports advanced spatio-temporal querying, including the highly efficient ASOF JOIN (As-Of Join)49. This specific extension allows the system to seamlessly and rapidly merge the continuous ![][image14] coordinate trajectories with the discrete, asynchronous scoring events predicted by ActionFormer. Teams can instantly execute complex SQL queries on the venue tablets, such as identifying a specific robot's average transit time from the Human Starting Line, through the Trenches, to the Alliance Hub under heavy defensive pressure6.

## **Ultra-Low-Bandwidth Client Synchronization (MQTT and Protobuf)**

The final architectural hurdle is delivering this immense wealth of analytical data to the strategy and scouting teams inside the competition venue. FRC venues (typically crowded high school gymnasiums or dense convention centers) are notorious for possessing some of the most congested, hostile Wi-Fi environments in the world. Attempting to stream video clips, or even heavy JSON payloads over HTTP, to tablets inside the venue will inevitably result in connection timeouts, packet loss, and disastrous data desynchronization5.  
To guarantee absolute delivery, the pipeline abandons traditional HTTP REST architectures and WebSockets with JSON payloads. Instead, the server-side Kubernetes cluster serializes the spatio-temporal telemetry, ActionFormer metadata, and reconciled scoring metrics using Protocol Buffers (Protobuf)4. Protobuf is a strongly typed, binary serialization format that strips away all the structural overhead (keys, brackets, whitespace) inherent in JSON, resulting in a drastically compressed, highly efficient payload54.

| Protocol & Serialization | Transport Layer | Average Payload Size | Network Resilience |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **HTTP \+ JSON** | TCP | \> 250 KB | Poor (Connection Drops) |
| **WebSockets \+ JSON** | TCP | \~ 180 KB | Moderate |
| **MQTT \+ Protobuf** | TCP (Pub/Sub) | **\< 50 KB** | **Excellent (Stateful)** |

These Protobuf binaries are transmitted via MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport)4. MQTT is a lightweight, publish-subscribe messaging protocol designed explicitly for high-latency, unreliable networks with severe bandwidth constraints5. It utilizes minimal TCP/IP overhead (as low as 2 bytes per message for QoS 0\) and maintains persistent, stateful connections that gracefully handle intermittent signal drops without requiring a full connection handshake upon reconnection56.  
By compressing the match trajectory data, action timestamps, and reconciled scoring metrics into a dense binary format, the entire match profile for all six robots is transmitted in a payload weighing less than 50KB55. The read-only venue tablets, running a lightweight DuckDB visualization client, subscribe to the MQTT broker, receiving instantaneous, authoritative updates the moment the Ray Core pipeline finishes processing the broadcast57.

## **Conclusion**

The 2026 REBUILT automated scouting pipeline represents a definitive paradigm shift in competitive FRC analytics. By eliminating the reliance on vulnerable field-side hardware and fully embracing the elasticity and power of a lab-hosted Kubernetes supercluster, the architecture achieves unprecedented resilience and uptime. The synthesis of Isaac Sim domain randomization, Ray Core distributed pipelining, and Levenberg-Marquardt optimized DLT homography effectively neutralizes the chaos and unreliability of public broadcast streams.  
Furthermore, the integration of BoT-SORT for camera-motion-compensated tracking and ActionFormer for multi-scale temporal action localization comprehensively solves the historical CV failure modes of visual occlusion and bounding-box identity switches. Backed by DuckDB's highly vectorized trajectory analytics and delivered through an ultra-low-bandwidth MQTT and Protobuf transport layer, this system provides strategic teams with infallible, granular data. Moving beyond the mathematical limitations of Component OPR, this architecture captures the true, nonlinear spatial dynamics of FRC match play, transforming raw broadcast pixels into a decisive, undeniable competitive advantage.
